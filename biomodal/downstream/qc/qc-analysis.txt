  ---
  Revised QC Assessment (Critical)

  Per-tile Sequence Quality: FAIL

  This is the most concerning flag. Both samples show the exact same bad tiles,
  confirming a flow cell defect:

  ┌──────┬──────────┬───────────────────┬───────────────────┐
  │ Tile │ Position │ Deviation (ctrl1) │ Deviation (ctrl2) │
  ├──────┼──────────┼───────────────────┼───────────────────┤
  │ 2350 │ base 9   │ -12.17            │ -12.60            │
  ├──────┼──────────┼───────────────────┼───────────────────┤
  │ 2245 │ base 9   │ -9.82             │ -9.95             │
  ├──────┼──────────┼───────────────────┼───────────────────┤
  │ 2441 │ base 8   │ -5.82             │ -6.24             │
  └──────┴──────────┴───────────────────┴───────────────────┘

  - 39 of 782 tiles (5%) have deviations below -2 at some position
  - 3 tiles below -5, 1 tile below -10
  - All problem tiles are on surface 2 (2xxx range), suggesting a localized
  defect on the bottom surface of the flow cell -- probably a focus issue,
  bubble, or manufacturing defect
  - The worst positions are early in the read (bases 8-9), with scattered issues
   at read ends (150-151bp) and mid-read positions
  - Because the same tiles fail identically across both samples, the data from
  those tiles is systematically degraded, not random noise. Reads from tile 2350
   in particular will have unreliable base calls at positions 8-9, which can
  cause misalignment and false methylation calls

  Impact: At 5% of tiles affected, this is a nontrivial fraction of your data.
  With the shallow coverage you have, you can't afford to throw away 5% of
  reads. But reads from these tiles (especially 2350 and 2245) will have
  elevated error rates that could introduce false positives in methylation
  calls, particularly at CHH sites where the true signal is near zero. Worth
  filtering or at least flagging.

  ---
  Per-sequence GC Content: WARN -- Poly-G Artifact

  The GC distribution peaks at 20%, which is expected for EM-seq-converted mouse
   genome. But there's a suspicious secondary peak at 95-100% GC:

  GC 95%:    5,648 reads
  GC 96%:    8,940 reads
  GC 97%:    7,517 reads
  GC 98%:   11,703 reads
  GC 99%:   28,950 reads
  GC 100%:  45,094 reads  <-- nearly 4x the GC 95% count

  This is a classic poly-G artifact from 2-color chemistry (NovaSeq/NextSeq).
  When clusters die or produce no fluorescent signal, the instrument calls G by
  default. These ~108K reads (0.33% of total) survived adapter trimming and
  mapping, meaning they aligned somewhere -- likely to G-rich repeats, telomeric
   sequences, or simple repeats in the reference.

  Impact: These reads are artifacts, not real data. At your shallow coverage,
  even a small number of spurious poly-G reads aligning to CpG islands or
  GC-rich promoter regions could inflate methylation estimates locally. fastp
  has a --trim_poly_g option (enabled by default for NextSeq/NovaSeq) -- but
  these reads survived, suggesting the poly-G trimming either wasn't aggressive
  enough or these are partial poly-G reads that slipped through. Worth checking
  if --trim_poly_g was explicitly enabled or if a stricter minimum length was
  used.

  ---
  Sequence Length Distribution: WARN

  - 86% of reads are full length (150-152bp)
  - 14% are trimmed to shorter lengths (adapter read-through)
  - 1.8% are very short (<80bp)
  - The trimmed fraction increases smoothly from ~0.01% at 30-34bp to ~1.6% at
  145-149bp

  The 14% adapter-trimmed fraction is on the higher side -- it means a
  meaningful fraction of fragments are shorter than the read length. This is
  consistent with the insert size mode of 192bp (many fragments are ~100-140bp,
  reading into adapters from both ends). This isn't catastrophic but it does
  mean:
  - You lose usable bases from those reads (mean read length drops from 151 to
  147bp)
  - Very short reads (<50bp) have lower mapping quality and higher mismatch
  rates
  - Some of these short fragments may be adapter dimers that survived filtering

  ---
  Per-base Sequence Content: Passed, but worth noting

  The base composition across the read body is:
  - T: ~39%, A: ~39%, G: ~11%, C: ~11%

  This reflects C→T conversion depleting cytosines from ~25% to ~11%. The
  residual 11% C includes:
  - CpG methylation (protected from conversion)
  - CHG/CHH methylation (very small)
  - Incomplete conversion (false negatives)

  The ratio is consistent across read positions (no obvious M-bias slope), which
   is good for EM-seq, but the 11% residual C is something to keep in mind when
  interpreting methylation levels.

  ---
  Things I Was Too Generous About Earlier

  1. Conversion efficiency: I quoted 98.9% from the lambda nonconversion rate,
  but that metric uses a lenient per-read threshold (likely flagging reads with
  >3 consecutive unconverted CHH). The more honest metric from the mbias CHH 
  data is 1.3-1.6% false-positive methylation in non-CpG context. Ideal EM-seq
  should be <1%. This is acceptable but not excellent.
  2. T4 and Xp12 controls: I mentioned they had 0 reads and moved on. This
  actually matters -- if your EM-seq kit included these spike-ins (T4 = 5hmC
  control, Xp12 = 5mC positive control), having zero reads means either:
    - They weren't added to the library (check your protocol/kit)
    - They were at negligibly low concentration
    - There's a reference indexing issue

  Without T4 you can't assess 5hmC detection sensitivity. Without Xp12 you lose
  an independent check on 5mC protection efficiency. Lambda and pUC19 alone give
   you conversion/protection rates, but T4 and Xp12 would give you a more
  complete picture.
  3. Coverage: ~1x is not just "shallow" -- it's insufficient for any CpG-level 
  methylation analysis. At 1x, the vast majority of CpGs will have 0 or 1 read
  covering them. You cannot make per-site methylation calls. This data is only
  useful for:
    - QC assessment (which is what we're doing)
    - Very coarse genome-wide average methylation estimates
    - Confirming the library prep worked before committing to deeper sequencing

  ---
  Bottom Line

  The library prep and enzymatic conversion look solid. The sequencing had real
  issues: a flow cell defect affecting ~5% of tiles and poly-G artifacts from
  the 2-color chemistry. Neither is a library problem -- both are
  instrument/flow cell problems. The data quality is adequate for QC purposes
  but you'll need much deeper sequencing (10-30x per sample) for actual
  methylation analysis, and when you do, consider:
  - Filtering reads from the worst tiles (2350, 2245, 2441)
  - Ensuring poly-G trimming is aggressive
  - Investigating whether T4/Xp12 controls should have been present
