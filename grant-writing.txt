  Location 1: Aim 1.1 (evoC, GRANT.md line 54)

  What makes the list (6 items):

  1. Name the tool and model. The Biomodal modality XPLR framework calls
     differentially methylated regions from the DUET evoC Zarr stores. The
     statistical model is binomial logistic regression with a Wald test on the
     group coefficient.
  2. Sex as covariate. The model includes sex as a covariate, matching the
     CUT&RUN and RNA-seq designs.
  3. Depth filter. CpG positions with mean coverage below 10 across all samples
     are excluded before region-level count aggregation. (This is from
     config_CG.txt: Depth_Filter=10.)
  4. Region classes tested. Six GENCODE vM25 annotation classes: gene bodies,
     promoters, CpG islands, CpG shores, CpG shelves, and TSS regions. Gene
     bodies are the primary comparison because the preliminary data (Figure 6,
     line 51) shows the methylation signal is a gene-body phenomenon.
  5. Independent 5mC and 5hmC testing, then joint interpretation. The model fits
     5mC and 5hmC separately for each region. Genes that reach significance in
     both contexts are classified by the sign of change in each: coordinated

     Aim 1 tests the cascade prediction. If the model is right, loci that gain
     H2Aub will show the coordinated pattern.

  Location 2: Aim 1.2 (EM-seq, GRANT.md line 55)

  The current text is one sentence. It states the rationale (mCH is neuronally
  enriched and DNMT3A-dependent) and the question (does mCH accrual track H2Aub
  redistribution?). It names Enzymatic Methyl-seq as the platform. It says
  nothing about how differential mCH is tested.

  What makes the list (6 items):

  1. Gene-body aggregation rationale. Site-level differential mCH testing is
     underpowered. Gene-body mCH rates (about 0.6-1.3%) sit near the per-sample
     false-positive rate from incomplete enzymatic conversion, measured at
     lambda phage CH sites (about 0.5-0.9%). Aggregating all CH sites within
     each protein-coding gene body (GENCODE vM25) pools enough observations to
     separate signal from noise.

      This rationale earns its space because it answers the reviewer question
     "why not test individual CpH sites?" The answer is biological and
     statistical: the signal is real but small, and you need gene-level pooling
     to detect it.
  2. edgeR quasi-likelihood F-test. Name the framework and cite Robinson et al.
     2010 and Lun et al. 2016 (both are in stats.md). The quasi-likelihood
     framework estimates per-gene dispersion, which is important when biological
     variance differs across genes.
  3. Design matrix: ~ genotype + sex + lambda_ch_rate. Three terms. Genotype is
     the treatment variable. Sex is a biological covariate (same as evoC and
     CUT&RUN). Lambda phage CH conversion rate is a technical covariate that
     absorbs between-sample variation in enzymatic conversion efficiency.
  4. Offset: log(total_coverage). Per-gene, per-sample. This accounts for
     differences in sequencing depth across genes and samples without
     library-size normalization. The grant already explains (Innovation section,
     line 32) that EM-seq preserves DNA integrity and achieves higher coverage
     than bisulfite. The offset is how that coverage enters the model.
  5. Lambda spike-in as covariate (not subtracted). This is the single most
     important methodological decision in the EM-seq pipeline. Lambda phage DNA
     is unmethylated. Any apparent CH methylation on lambda is conversion noise.
     The per-sample conversion rate enters the model as a covariate.
     Pre-subtraction of counts zeros out too many genes when the signal is this
     close to the noise floor.

      One sentence on the approach, one on the rationale. The grant already uses
     a similar level of method justification elsewhere (for example, the
     Innovation section at line 32 explains why EM-seq is better than bisulfite
     at length).
  6. All-CH (CHH + CHG combined) as primary analysis. Combining CHH and CHG
     dinucleotides gives about 3 times more sites per gene body than CA
     dinucleotides alone. This increases statistical power for the gene-body
     aggregation. A parallel CA-only analysis exists for comparison with
     published mCA atlases (Luo et al. 2017).

      I go back and forth on whether to include the CA parallel. The grant's
     Innovation section (line 26) specifically discusses non-CG methylation at
     CA dinucleotides. If the grant claims mCA is the biologically interesting
     mark, a reviewer will ask why the primary analysis uses all-CH instead of
     CA-only. The answer (statistical power from 3x more sites) is simple and
     worth stating.

  What stays out:

  - Current numbers. Same reasoning as Aim 1.1. The 5,557 significant genes, the
    7.7:1 direction ratio, the genomic inflation factor, the top gene (Auts2):
    all results, not methods.
  - Per-sample lambda rates. Implementation detail. The grant describes the
    approach (lambda as covariate). The specific rates per sample belong in a
    paper supplement.
  - Robust dispersion estimation. The R script uses estimateDisp(y, design, 
    robust = TRUE). The robust = TRUE flag is a good default but is not a
    methodological decision worth explaining in a grant aim. "edgeR
    quasi-likelihood F-test" is enough.
  - Gene-level filtering thresholds (100 CH sites minimum, 2500 total coverage 
    minimum). These are reasonable defaults from the script
    (03_mch_differential.R, lines 61-65). A grant aim paragraph does not need to
    specify filtering cutoffs at this resolution. If a reviewer asks, these go
    in a response-to-reviewers or a paper methods section.

  Presentation reasoning:

  The existing sentence stays as the opener (rationale + question). The new
  material goes after it: 3-4 sentences describing gene-body aggregation, the
  edgeR model, and the all-CH choice. The paragraph then reads: "Here is why mCH
  matters. Here is the platform. Here is how we test for differential mCH. Here
  is what we ask."

  Cross-cutting questions for you

  1. The n = 4 vs n >= 6 discrepancy.

  The Rigor section (line 44) says "we will maintain n >= 6 per genotype per
  timepoint." The current evoC and EM-seq data both have n = 4 per genotype.
  Stats.md flags this as unresolved.

  This matters for the aim paragraphs because some model features depend on
  sample size. At n = 4 per genotype with a sex covariate, you have 2 per sex
  per genotype. At n = 6, you have 3. The model formula works either way, but
  statistical power changes.

  I will not write around this or pick a number. But you need to know: if the
  aim text describes "sex as a covariate in the model" and the Rigor section
  promises n >= 6, a reviewer will note that n >= 6 with sex as a covariate
  means 3 per sex per genotype at minimum. That arithmetic is consistent. The
  issue is only that the preliminary data used n = 4. If the preliminary data is
  presented as preliminary (and it is, in the Preliminary Studies section),
  this is fine. The aim describes the proposed analysis at the proposed sample
  size.

  Do you want me to flag this for Cole, or do you already have a resolution?

  2. How much model rationale goes in the aim paragraphs vs. Rigor?

  The grant has a convention. The aim paragraphs state what you will do and what
  you will ask. The Rigor section states the statistical safeguards. Right now
  my plan puts the model descriptions (binomial logistic regression for evoC,
  edgeR QL for EM-seq) in the aim paragraphs, and the tool names plus citations
  in Rigor.

  The alternative: put only the conceptual approach in the aims ("we will call
  differentially methylated regions with sex as a covariate and test 5mC and
  5hmC independently") and put the model specification in Rigor.

  I think the aims need the model named, because the model choice is part of the
  analytical logic. A reviewer reads the aim to understand what you will do.
  "We will call DMRs" is not enough. "We will call DMRs by binomial logistic
  regression" tells the reviewer what the statistical test is. The Rigor section
  then names the software and the citations.

  But this is a judgment call about grant writing conventions. Your instinct?

  3. The coordinated-change classification: full explanation or one sentence?

  The coordinated-change classification (join 5mC and 5hmC results per gene,
  classify by direction) is central to how Aim 1.1 tests the cascade hypothesis.
  If loci that gain H2Aub show coordinated 5mC gain + 5hmC loss, that supports
  the TET-impediment model. If they show only 5mC gain without 5hmC loss, that
  supports DNMT3A recruitment.

  This means the coordinated classification is not just a statistical method. It
  is an interpretive framework tied to the hypothesis. I think it earns 2
  sentences in the aim: one stating the classification, one stating the
  prediction.

  But I want your read on this. Do you see the coordinated-change classification
  as method (goes in a methods description) or as hypothesis-testing logic
  (goes in the aim narrative)?

  4. One thing I am skeptical of.

  The grant's Innovation section (line 26) frames the EM-seq mCH analysis as

  between genotypes at a single timepoint per comparison. It does not directly
  test whether H2Aub causes mCH redistribution. The causal test is in Aim 1.3

  This is fine for the grant. Aim 1.2 asks "does mCH change where H2Aub

  analytical detail you add to Aim 1.2 describes a per-timepoint differential
  test, not a causal test. The wording of the aim paragraph needs to match that.
  The current wording ("determine whether mCH accrual is redirected") is fine
  as a question. The statistical description just needs to describe what the
  test actually tests: differential gene-body mCH between Bap1 mutant and
  control at each timepoint.

  This is not something to change. It is something to keep in mind as you write,
  so the statistical description matches the question the aim actually asks.

  Good question. The core problem: you have 4 timepoints (P7, P12, P21, adult)  
  and you want to test whether H2Aub changes happen *before* DNMT3A/methylation 
  changes, not just *with* them. Here are the real options, concretely.         
                                                                                
  **1. Onset classification (simplest)**                                        
                                                                                
  Run the differential analysis independently at each timepoint. For each gene, 
  record the earliest timepoint where each mark (H2Aub, DNMT3A, 5mC, 5hmC)      
  first reaches significance between mutant and control. Then ask: do genes     
  with early H2Aub onset (say, significant at P12) tend to show early or late   
  methylation onset? A contingency table and Fisher's test.                     
                                                                                
  What this looks like in practice: a 3×3 table (onset at P12 / P21 / adult)    
  for H2Aub vs methylation, testing whether the ordering is non-random. The     
  prediction is that H2Aub onset is systematically earlier than methylation     
  onset at the same genes.                                                      
                                                                                
  **2. Cross-correlation of fold-change trajectories (what the grant names)**   
                                                                                
  For each gene, compute the mutant-vs-control fold change for each mark at     
  each timepoint. That gives you a 4-point trajectory per mark per gene.        
  Cross-correlation at lag 0 asks: do the H2Aub and methylation trajectories    
  move together at the same timepoints? At lag 1: does the H2Aub trajectory at  
  time *t* correlate with the methylation trajectory at time *t+1*?             
                                                                                
  The catch: 4 timepoints is very few for this. Any single gene's               
  cross-correlation is noisy. The power comes from aggregating across thousands 
  of genes — if the average cross-correlation peaks at lag 1 rather than lag 0  
  across the genome, that's evidence that methylation follows H2Aub by one      
  developmental window.                                                         
                                                                                
  **3. Predictive regression (Granger-like)**                                   
                                                                                
  Ask: does the H2Aub fold change at timepoint *t* predict the DNMT3A fold      
  change at timepoint *t+1*, after you control for DNMT3A's own value at time   
  *t*?                                                                          
                                                                                
  Concretely: `DNMT3A_fold(t+1) ~ DNMT3A_fold(t) + H2Aub_fold(t)`. If the H2Aub 
  coefficient is significant, that means knowing where H2Aub changed tells you  
  where DNMT3A will change next, beyond what DNMT3A's own trajectory tells      
  you. You'd pool across genes (thousands of gene × 3 transitions each).        
                                                                                
  This is the most formal version of "does X lead Y." Classical Granger         
  causality needs many more timepoints, but pooling across genes partially      
  compensates.                                                                  
                                                                                
  **4. Ordered-event test**                                                     
                                                                                
  For each gene, define events: the first timepoint each mark becomes           
  significant. Then count what fraction of genes show the predicted cascade     
  ordering (H2Aub first → DNMT3A second → methylation third). Compare to chance
