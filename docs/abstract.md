## Summary: Writing a Conference Abstract (UCSD URH Workshop)

This workshop, led by Dr. Marie Cinnamon and Dr. Sophia Steinery from UCSD's Undergraduate Research Hub, covers the structure, criteria, and best practices for writing a conference abstract.

### Abstract Structure

An abstract is a brief, clear summary designed to help readers quickly understand your research and decide whether to attend your presentation. It follows four core components:

**Introduction (1–3 sentences):** State your research question and why it matters — a gap in the literature, potential impact, etc. **Methodology/Theoretical Framework (1–3 sentences):** Describe how you're conducting the research — data collection, analysis methods, and any theoretical lens (e.g., critical race theory, feminist theory). **Results:** Summarize actual, preliminary, or expected findings. Submitting before you have results is common and acceptable at undergraduate conferences. **Conclusion/Significance (optional but recommended):** Explain what your results suggest, their implications, and future directions. Treated as "extra credit" if space allows.

### The Four C's and Common Pitfalls

A strong abstract is **complete** (covers all major parts), **concise** (no excess words), **clear** (minimal jargon, readable by non-specialists), and **cohesive** (flows smoothly rather than reading as disconnected bullet points). Don't include the presentation title, author names, or citations in the abstract body — those go elsewhere. Avoid first-person narration like "it is my opinion that" or "I have concluded."

### Practical Reminders

Follow the conference's specific word limits and formatting directions. Proofread carefully since abstracts are often published as-is. Have your research mentor review it for accuracy and to avoid disclosing proprietary information. Spell out acronyms on first use, but only if you'll reference them again — otherwise it wastes space.

### On Balancing Jargon vs. Specificity

The workshop emphasizes tailoring jargon to your audience. A specialist conference tolerates more technical language, but for broad venues like undergraduate research conferences, lean toward accessibility. The introduction and significance sections can usually stay jargon-free, while methodology may require more technical terms. A good litmus test: could you explain this to a family member who isn't in your field?

### Three Worked Examples

The presenters walked through three abstracts of increasing quality — from a bare-bones one-sentence abstract missing results, to a social sciences abstract with good content but choppy organization, to a well-structured PAAD (pancreatic cancer) bioinformatics abstract that flowed clearly through all four components. The progression illustrated how structure and flow distinguish a serviceable abstract from a strong one.
