## The Poster as a Visual Abstract

So the basic idea of a poster is that the poster itself is really a visual illustration of an abstract, right? So if you were writing up the basic outline of the research that you're doing, the abstract is the most condensed form of that, right? The abstract in any paper will give you the key findings, the basic methodology that was done, and the guiding research question that was attempted to be answered, all within about 200 words. And what we're doing is basically transforming that abstract into a visual presentation of the same information, right?

---

## Tell a Story

So the big idea here is that what you want to think about is your poster needs to tell a story, right? Anybody that — I was going to say walks by your poster, but they're not going to be virtually walking by it, right? They need to be able to look at your poster at a cursory glance and get the general gist of what you're doing, right? So you want to be able to convey that message very quickly and very visually in your poster.

---

## Make It Visual

And that brings me to my second point: make it visual. A lot of people, myself included, are very word-forward on posters. That is generally not best practices here. You would want to make your poster as visually appealing as possible. If you have data, if you have illustrations, if you have examples of methodologies that were used or equipment that was used, et cetera, try and implement any kind of visual representation in place of a sentence, right? The more information you can present visually, again, the easier your story is to tell to the viewer, especially in this kind of platform where we're going to be presenting in Zoom. You want people to be able to look at your poster and take away the main message rather quickly.

---

## Less Is More

The other thing is to consider: less is more, right, in the poster presentation. Again, I am just as bad at this as everybody else, but I tend to put way too much information on the poster, way too many words on my posters, and they're more difficult to understand and digest in that kind of format.

So if anything, I would advise you to go through a couple rounds of editing, and we'll talk a little bit about this again at the end. Put everything that you think is important down and then come back to it and take some stuff away, right? The idea is that, again, your poster needs to tell the big picture, the broad story, and then you're going to be there, right? You get to fill in the details as part of your presentation, as part of your response to questions. So not every single detail needs to be on your poster. You want enough information so that people understand what you're doing and how you did it and what the take-home message is from your work, but all of the minute details are not necessary on your poster, right? So definitely less is more.

---

## Make It Interesting

Last but not least, I know that your research is fascinating to you — hopefully it is — but it may not come across that way for somebody who is not necessarily well-steeped in your research area, right? So you need to find a way to compel other people who may not be inherently interested in your research area to come and ask questions. You want to use your poster to draw people in and to get them invested, to get them excited about your science. And so you want to think about ways that you can increase visual interest. You can increase motivational interest to ask questions about your work, to share your findings, and to share your excitement about what you're doing, right? So think about ways to make it interesting for the viewer.

## Choosing Your Platform

First step: figure out what platform you're going to work in. My suggestion would be to use what you know, right? There are two basic poster software programs that people generally use.

The first is PowerPoint. PowerPoint is probably the most common platform that people will use to put together a poster. Why? Because they're already familiar with it, which means that it's generally easy to use. People are familiar with the interface and they're not trying to learn the program as they go. The idea of a PowerPoint though is that it's designed for a presentation or a projection, which is good in this case because we are going to be presenting it in this presentation mode.

However, for a traditional poster where you're going to be printing off the poster and presenting it in person, the presentation format of the PowerPoint isn't always the best resolution for images in particular. And it also has some issues if you try and use the file. As Heidi said earlier, usually you want to try and save it as a PDF if you're going to send it to the printer, because depending on what computer is opening the file, things might shift around and move. And then that can become distracting because it looks like a mistake. So there are some design issues. It's not actually designed to make posters, but it works quite well.

The colors and graphics can be deceiving when you're using PowerPoint. Particularly, again, if you are going to print these things out later on, the colors might show up a little bit different depending on the resolution of the computer that is used to print that poster. So again, the best practice here is generally to save it as a PDF to do the presentation, and if you were going to send it off to a printer, to send the PDF version off to the printer.

The other main presentation platform that people generally use is Adobe Illustrator or another one called InDesign. These are specifically designed software packages for poster presentations or for making presentations in a visual medium like this. There is a higher or steeper learning curve associated with these platforms because they're usually not as familiar to people. And therefore you are going to spend some time learning how to use the software just from the get-go. However, they are much higher quality graphics that are associated with these platforms. So essentially when you go to print it off, you essentially get exactly what you were seeing on your computer rather than some variation that might occur with the PowerPoint version.

Now, there are other platforms — Canva, PublishIt, Corel, LaTeX, et cetera, et cetera. So there are a variety of different ones. These two though, Adobe Illustrator or the PowerPoint, would probably be the most common ways that people are going to put their posters together. So I would recommend choosing one of those two. Again, whatever you're more familiar with, use that.

## Poster Size & Layout

So let's take a little detour then and talk about once you have a platform, what to put where. Some do's and don'ts.

So the first is to figure out how big your poster needs to be. That fortunately for this presentation is not super important, but generally at a national meeting, for example, there are boards that you would be tacking your poster up to and those boards are a certain size. So it's important that your poster fit in the space that's provided for the poster. Generally, the posters — or that size — is something that would be told to you by the meeting organizers. They would tell you your poster can't exceed a certain size. Usually though, a poster is about three feet by about five feet, somewhere in that range.

And so essentially what you could do is create one PowerPoint slide. You can go into the page settings for that PowerPoint slide and change it from eight and a half by eleven, which is what it would print out normally as one piece of paper. You can change then the size to be three feet by five feet, for example.

The other thing to consider is the general layout of your poster. I would say most posters are presented in landscape, or at least the ones that I see regularly are presented in landscape format, such as the poster in the upper left-hand corner over here. Otherwise, you could present a poster in a portrait format. There is no right way of doing this. It's really the way that is going to best communicate your information in the way that makes the most sense.

## Backgrounds & Colors

Once you have a layout, the next thing to consider is what goes behind everything. In general, I would say avoid dark backgrounds. If you really have your heart set on using a dark background, then make sure that you use light text so that you can actually see the information. In general, though, I would recommend using a lighter background for posters and therefore using darker text and images on the light background. It provides much more contrast for people to be able to easily read information — not just the verbal information presented in the text of your poster, but also the figures and data sets and other images that you might include on your poster. That, and it saves a lot of ink. So I would recommend setting up your poster with a light background.

The other thing to consider with your background: sometimes people like to put an image as part of the background. That's fine, as long as your image doesn't distract from the poster itself, right? You don't want the background of your poster to be the thing that everybody's focusing on. You want the information that's being presented on that background to be the focus of everybody's attention, right? So you're welcome to use a background that has some visual interest to it. You don't want it to be overly distracting. So if you have some kind of image, generally we would recommend that you increase the transparency of that image so that it's not so noticeable but still increases the visual appeal of your general poster.

Be careful with colors. So in general, I would say avoid primary colors — red, yellow, and blue — or combinations of those primary colors. In particular, red text on a blue background appears blurry to the human eye. If you try and read that, it's actually quite difficult to focus your eye on the red text without it appearing blurry to you, and therefore it's going to be difficult to read, and therefore it's going to be hard to communicate your message. Blue on red is even worse, right? So definitely avoid these color combinations.

And if you're using a light background, avoid yellow. Yellow on white, you can't really read at all. Remember, what you want is high contrast. You would want something that is very dark on a very light background, or very light on a very dark background. So be careful with the color choices that you're making, making sure that it's easy to understand and read, and that those colors don't clash with each other in a way that's going to be distracting.

I will also mention at this point: your lab might have a preferred format for posters. So this might be something to discuss with the graduate students in the lab that you're working in, or maybe the PI of the lab or postdoc in the lab. They might already have a template for how posters from your research group get presented, and a lot of these decisions about backgrounds and formatting and layouts and colors — those might be things that have already been decided. So that's definitely an easy way to go about not having to worry about making these kinds of decisions. But if you have the flexibility to create your own background, your own poster, your own color choices, definitely make wise decisions here.

## Essential Poster Sections

What information has to be on your poster? So there are a few pieces of important information that have to be — well, nothing *has* to be on your poster — but these are the things that I would highly recommend that you include on any poster.

Your poster should have a **title**, right? This is the thing that is going to get people's attention. So your title really needs to communicate the big picture of what you're trying to convey on your poster.

The next most important thing is **you**, the author of your poster. Other people that you need to consider for authorship would be anybody else that has added intellectual value and merit to the work that you're presenting. So this might be other students in the lab that are working on this part of the project with you. This might be other graduate students or postdocs in the lab, and it is definitely important to include your PI on your poster as one of the authors. Because this again is representing the body of work that's being presented from what you're doing.

The next thing that you would want to indicate is **affiliations** for those individual people. So a lot of the people might be from the same lab, but you might have contributors from different labs or maybe different institutions. And so you want to make sure that you acknowledge the affiliation of each one of those people — which department they're coming from, which institution they might be from.

The **introduction** section of your poster contains relevant background information. This is essentially going to be a small intro to your poster. This doesn't need to be everything about the research that you're working on. You want your introduction to be very focused on the work that's being presented so that people have a general understanding of what they're looking at.

The next important section to include is your **methodology**. How did you go about doing the work that you're presenting? Again, this doesn't need to be the methodology from the entire project that you're working on. If you're presenting a certain data set, then maybe you only focus your methodology on how that data set was arrived at.

The next section would be the **results** section. This is probably one of the most important parts of your poster. This is essentially communicating what you found, right? So this is your data. These might be graphs. They might be image files. If you are, let's say, looking at cell cultures or some sort of staining or something of that impact, you want to be able to include those kinds of images as part of your results section.

The **conclusions** are exactly what they suggest. These are the implications of your work — essentially what you found and the take-home message that you want to convey.

Any work that you are referencing on your poster — so this might be references to the methodologies that were used, the relevant background information — those **references** should be included on your poster.

And then the last section is usually some section about **acknowledgements** — so thanking either participants in the research that you might be working on or other people in the lab that have contributed that maybe aren't listed as authors but you still want to recognize and thank for their contributions. So any good poster is going to have all of these sections.

## Crafting Your Title

So let's focus on your title for a second, because again, this is the thing that should be grabbing most people's attention on a traditional poster. Quite often, people will try to inject humor into their titles, which is not inappropriate, but you want to do it carefully.

And I would say definitely avoid using colons. You'll see this a lot in posters where your poster will have some sort of title and then maybe a funny witty title after it, right? So you want to avoid those kinds of interjections with colons. You don't want your title to be long and rambling. You want your title to be straight to the point so that people understand what they're looking at. Also, people generally use colons to try and clarify. Remember, that's what you were there for. You get to clarify by all the things that you were saying during your poster presentation, so that doesn't need to be part of your title. Again, humor and clarity — you can definitely interject those into your title without using a grammatical crutch like a colon. And whatever you do, just don't use more than one colon. If you're going to use one, definitely don't use two or more.

Your title should be presented in what's called **sentence case**. So sentence case is how you would traditionally write a sentence where your first word is capitalized and any proper nouns are capitalized, but all other words are lowercase. And generally, your title does not have a period at the end, even though it's presented in sentence case. Interestingly, you do not use title case for a title in this context of a scientific poster presentation. Title case would be where you capitalize basically everything. That is not used for scientific presentations. And again, God forbid, don't use all caps, right? You're not screaming at people on your title. So you're using a general sentence case structure for your title.

## Fonts: Sans-Serif vs. Serif

You may or may not know this, but there are two different kinds of fonts in the world. There are what are called **sans-serif** fonts and **serif** fonts.

So serifs are the little flutes at the end of letters sometimes. So if you look at the bottom text here in Palatino — if you look, for example, at the P, there's this little section at the bottom of the P and a little — these are called flutes — a flute at the top of the P. And then if you look across letters, all of the letters have some version of that. Those are the serif fonts. Something like Helvetica or other kinds of fonts that don't have those kinds of embellishments on the letters are known as sans-serif, so without the flutes.

And those two kinds of texts serve two different kinds of purposes on your poster. Generally, it's recommended that you use sans-serif fonts for headings and titles, things that are going to be large and getting people's attention. If you have smaller bodies of text — so for example, sentences that you might write out in your introduction or in your conclusion section — you generally would be advised to use serif fonts for those kinds of presentations.

The idea is that those texts, what's called the body of text, is going to be easier to read with serif fonts because those little flutes create a visual line that allows the reader to read across without them moving up or down from one line of text to the next, right? Because generally, this information is smaller on your poster and the size makes it a little bit more difficult to read. The serif fonts will make it easier for them to read across without losing their place on your poster. So consider what kind of font you're using where and what the intent of that font would be.

There is no specific fonts that you need to use. Generally, we would recommend avoiding things that are like Comic Sans or other sort of more artistic kinds of fonts. You want them to be easy to read and legible from a distance generally. Remember, normally this is all happening in a physical space, and you want somebody who's just walking by to still be able to read what your poster is about.

## Headings & Font Sizes

For headings on your poster, don't use bullets for headings. You can create bulleted lists, but usually those are going to happen within a section. Your heading itself should just be a larger font than the text underneath that heading. And then if you have subheadings to maybe organize the information, we would definitely recommend that you use subheadings to do that organizational structure. You want to make them a little bit smaller so that it's indicative of the importance of that heading or that you can indicate that it falls underneath a major heading. And then within those headings, again, you can put in individual text or data files or images or the like.

Now, font size is something to consider. Again, if you're printing this out and you have a three-foot by five-foot poster, if you were typing everything in size 12 font, it's going to be extremely tiny. So these are not hard and fast sizes. These are just general categories for you to consider.

Again, thinking about what's important on your poster and what you want to make easily visible on your poster: your title should probably be the largest thing. I would say no smaller than size 80 point font. You want it to be easily read from across the room, essentially. And in this case, because your poster is going to be presented in this Zoom format, you are going to potentially have to move in or out by zooming in or out of your poster. So again, you want the most important information to be clearly visible without you having to zoom in on it.

So your title, for example, you want it to be quite large so that it's easily read by any individual in the space. Authors are probably the next most important thing. You did all the work and you need to get some credit for that. Again, I would recommend probably no smaller than size 54 point font. Headings would be the next section — so these would be like your Introduction, your Results, your Methods, those kinds of sections on your poster. Headings should be the next most important section. Again, probably no smaller than size 36 point font. Any sentences or body text that's in your poster, around size 24 point font. And then references or captions to things or other sort of less important but necessary details, I would put in relatively small font, about size 18 point font.

Again, think about trying to structure your poster such that your most important information is being communicated easily from across the space. And so again, this is now a virtual space that we're talking about, but it's the same basic idea. You want somebody looking at their computer screen to not have to squint and move close to just figure out what you're talking about.

## Presenting Your Data

Let's take a minute and talk about presenting your data. So depending on the kind of data — consider what details you can remove or simplify in your visual representations to make them easier to understand. So instead of having, for example, days by every two days indicated, you can remove a lot of that extra information that's really not necessary. So you get the same information on the right — you still have the same 20 days overall that's being represented, but there's less distracting numbers for you to pay attention to, but still conveying the same information. So consider what you can remove or what you can make less burdensome for the viewer to try and understand what you're showing.

The other thing to indicate — and you see this quite frequently on graphical representations — is that a legend will be displayed separate from the actual graphical representation. So on the left, for example, the predator and prey notation for the legend is represented separately from the actual lines that indicate the predator and prey populations. On the right, it's indicated that the prey is still the green by putting the label "prey" next to that particular line, and the predator population is still the pink one, but now we've just indicated it by integrating the legend with the graphical representation.

This is known as the **split attention effect**. So if I have to spend time looking at your graph, looking at a legend, looking back at the graph, and then back at the legend to make sure that I understand everything, that takes a lot of cognitive time and effort. So we want to try and help the viewer out as much as possible and try to integrate those pieces of information with each other as closely as we can. So in the representation on the right, that creates less cognitive overload for the viewer to try and understand what you're showing, while still conveying the same information. One population is going down, one population is going up, and we've tried to essentially strip away all the extraneous information to make it as easy for the viewer to understand as possible.

The other thing I would say is make everything as big as possible. Not that we're trying to just fill up the space here, but again, you want to make it easy to understand and read. And again, this is both important in the visual format but also in the virtual format. If you need to zoom in quite a bit, that might be distracting from your overall presentation. And so if you don't need to do that by making your images large enough to understand in the first place, then that just cuts down on the sort of negotiation that you have to do to best convey your material.

## The 800-Word Rule

This is probably the one mistake that I make constantly — I put too many words on my poster, right? If your poster has more than 800 words on it, you're doing it wrong, right? And I say this as somebody who constantly does this wrong. And I think maybe this is just — scientists like to talk about their work, right? They like to talk about their research and they generally are very verbose about what they want to convey.

Remember, you don't need to put everything on the poster, right? Make sure that everything that's on there is essential information, and all the little details and fun stories or nuances to what you're working on — you can provide that during the presentation, right? When you're talking, when you're answering questions, you can fill in all those gaps.

You do want your poster to be able to be understood if you weren't there, right? So somebody who just walked by your poster or views your poster without you in the space should still be able to get the general understanding of what you're doing. They should still be able to understand the take-home message from your poster and the basic way in which that information was arrived at. But they don't need all the little details. If they're curious, they can reach out to you and ask you those questions, right?

So I would avoid large blocks of text on your poster, particularly in this virtual environment. If you're presented just with a wall of text, quite often people just tune out, right? They stop paying attention because it's just too overwhelming, right? You want to be able to focus their attention to the most important pieces of information and you can't do that by presenting everything all at once, right? So brevity here is really an important key asset to creating a good poster. Less is more.

## Get Feedback & Iterate

All right, so that brings us to this point. There's no perfect poster that would be created just sort of initially, right? Your poster is a communication tool. It's a way of presenting information. And usually this is gonna take several rounds of iteration, right?

So if you already know what you're going to be presenting your poster on, what I would recommend is for you to start by putting together a rough draft of your poster and then send it around to a few friends, to maybe some other people in your lab, maybe your PI, and get feedback, right? Do they understand what your poster is about? Do they agree that the way that it's being presented is the most efficient and effective way of communicating that information? Quite often you'll get a lot of really great feedback from other people that are working on your project with you just about the best ways of presenting this information, the best ways to convey certain kinds of ideas, appropriate kind of graphical information, visual information, et cetera, right? So definitely take the time to get feedback, right? Build that into your timeline for making your poster — to engage in at least one round of feedback or editing, right?

The other group that might be really important to send this to is maybe a friend or two who has no idea what you do. You want to try and see — can somebody who doesn't understand the research that you're working on, can they still understand basically what you did, right? Do they get the big picture, right? And they're a really interesting test group to get feedback from because they'll be able to point out things that people in the discipline might just sort of accept as the normal way that we would present information. So they might ask some really great questions to help clarify how you're presenting your work, right? So definitely take the time to get feedback.

## Poster 2.0

So at this point, I do want to mention that what I've been talking about is this traditional poster format, right? Where everything is being presented on the same space. However, more recently, there is an updated poster format. So this is known as **Poster 2.0**. You may have heard about it over the last year or two. It's really started to gain traction. I would say in my experience, the posters that I've seen at national conferences and other venues are still traditionally the normal poster that I just went through. But you are starting to see these Poster 2.0 formats increasing in popularity.

The basic idea of Poster 2.0 is that scientists are usually really bad at communicating with the general public about their work, right? They're really good at talking to other scientists, especially scientists in their discipline, about their work. But you want your work to be able to be understood by everybody, not just people who work in your field with your specific expertise.

So the basic idea of Poster 2.0 is that that center portion of your poster — where most people are going to focus their attention — that center portion on a traditional poster is usually like your results section, other image files, relevant data or graphs. In the Poster 2.0 format, it's usually a single sentence, very large text. And that is the main finding of your work, right? So that is the entire section. It's mostly empty space. And the idea is to really hammer home what is the main idea from your work, right? This is generally presented in non-jargon language so that anybody could understand the ideas that you're trying to communicate — that this main finding is the thing that you want them to walk away with.

On the outsides of that middle section, you'll see some of the traditional sections that we talked about previously. So you'll still have a title and authors and an introduction, methods and research and discussion. Maybe there's some extra information on the right as well — maybe some extra figures or tables and the like. And usually these Poster 2.0 structures include some kind of QR code on them somewhere. And the idea is that that QR code could take you to a webpage that actually has a lot more detail on it — that the whole point of the poster is really just to draw people in. And then if they want to investigate more, then they have applications to do that, right?

So I would say if you are interested in structuring your poster presentation using this Poster 2.0 format, you're welcome to do that. Again, there is no right way to make a poster. I would say though that the traditional poster format that we've gone through previously is probably the more common structure that you're going to run into. And your PI might have strong feelings about one or the other of these kinds of formats. So I would definitely get feedback before you do any major design element here to think about what would be the best way of communicating your work.

## Know Your Audience

All right, last but not least, the audience is important, right? So traditionally, poster presentations would be happening usually in a large convention hall, right? So there might be hundreds or even thousands of people all in the same space and rows and rows of posters, basically, and people walking up and down in these rows. Now, in this virtual symposium format, it's a little bit different, but the idea is essentially the same. Your audience is going to be made up of different kinds of people, and so it's important to know who you're talking to, right?

So there are a few different kinds of people in that space, right? In general, I would say there are people that are there just because they're interested, right? So these might be people from your lab, friends. This might be people from the department that are just curious to see what you're doing. These people are easy to talk to because they're generally interested in what you're doing. They might come up and ask questions, so there might be during the virtual symposium just opportunities for them to engage and ask questions about your work and what you're doing. You don't really have to do a lot of work here. They're going to come to you. They're going to ask you questions. You just need to respond to them.

In a traditional poster section, there are also people that I call **the lurkers**. These are people that stand maybe like three or five feet away from your poster. They don't come up to you. They don't ask questions. They might just stare at your poster, maybe stare at you for a little while, and then they wander away without ever talking to you, right? And that's not uncommon. That might also happen in this virtual symposium where you just have people that are just there, right? It doesn't mean that they're not interested, but they're not coming up and actively asking you questions. They're just there and paying attention. They don't want you to launch into a huge explanation about something unless they ask you a question, right? So they're just curious. They're perusing. They're looking at stuff. And the general advice that I give students is to let them be. They're going to be there. They might stare at you. It might be a little bit awkward. That's fine, right?

The last group that's important for you are **the judges**, right? So there will be judges in the virtual symposium session. There are judges in the traditional poster sessions as well. These are people that, like myself, who might be faculty members in the department. You'll be judged by usually faculty members within the same discipline that your work is being presented from. There are also industry-invited judges, so people who work in industrial settings that might do relevant research or activities related to your research.

And so these are generally people with expertise in the area. They might ask questions about some technicalities, about methodologies, about possible implications of your work or ways that your work might be utilized or next steps for what you're doing. And so they're going to ask maybe more technical — I wouldn't say necessarily harder questions — but they're going to ask more nuanced questions about your work and how it's being done and what it means. And so I would definitely be prepared to talk to any one of these three kinds of people in that space.

## Be Concise in Your Answers

For all of them, though, I would say that it's important that you consider being concise in your answers. If they want more information, they will ask follow-up questions.

The problem — and this is maybe not such a problem in a virtual space, but it is definitely a problem in a physical space — is that during poster sessions, you'll see somebody who's very excited to talk about their work, and they will launch into a five-, ten-minute-long just monologue, right?
